## Core Product Identity and Agent-Driven Non-Linear Editing

Bench is built as a specialized desktop video editor for macOS that interfaces directly with coding-centric artificial intelligence agents, specifically Claude Code and Codex. Unlike cloud-based generative tools that produce synthetic video clips from text prompts, Bench works directly with authentic source footage imported by the editor. The software positions the human creator as the director and the connected AI agent as the technical assistant. Rather than manually hunting through multi-angle sequences to adjust interview cadences or drag timeline keyframes, the editor issues plain natural language instructions, visual sketches, or pointing gestures, which the external agent translates into timeline actions.

The interaction model combines visual pointing with written or spoken commands. An editor can click directly on a subject in the video display to identify who should remain in frame, draw a rough travel path or bounding box on the picture to show where elements should go, or type an instruction such as tightening an interview sequence while preserving natural laughter. Because Bench is structured as a full desktop editor, it preserves traditional timeline views, audio waveforms, and visual scopes. Creators retain the ability to step in, make manual frame-by-frame cuts or color corrections, and allow the agent to resume work from wherever the human operator leaves it, ensuring that automated assistance never prevents direct manual control.

This architectural choice differentiates the software from closed automation black boxes. Users are not handed an immutable final render. Instead, they receive editable sequence modifications inside a workspace that still allows manual trim adjustments, volume keyframe placement, and color balance fine-tuning. For video production teams, this distinction is critical because client delivery standards almost always require minute manual adjustments before final master export.


## Supervised Automation and the Approval-First Gate

A primary concern for professional editors when considering automated software tools is the danger of destructive sequence overwrites. In a high-stakes post-production workflow, an automated script that scrambles audio sync, clips dialogue tails, or ruins an established color balance introduces hours of troubleshooting. Bench counters this risk by embedding an approval gate into every automated interaction. According to the publisher, no timeline change, color adjustment, or audio filter lands permanently on the master edit sequence until the human user reviews and explicitly confirms the modification.

When an agent finishes processing a command—such as lowering background music underneath a speaker's voice or applying a warm golden-hour grading pass across a scene—the result is presented inside an isolated preview mode. The user can inspect the proposed adjustment against their existing cut, determine whether the pacing and levels match expectations, and make an informed editorial call. The interface provides explicit controls to accept the modification, request a revised pass with refined parameters, or discard the suggestion entirely. Because undo functionality remains accessible at all times, editors maintain supervisory oversight over every creative iteration without risking project corruption.

From an operational perspective, this preview-first model acts as an internal quality checkpoint. It shifts the role of the AI agent from an autonomous operator to an obedient junior assistant whose drafts must pass human inspection. Whether this preview mechanism introduces timeline lag during complex multi-layer sequences remains an open question that each editor must assess during their own workflow evaluation.


## Hardware Boundaries, Operating System Rules, and Offline Licensing

Technical teams auditing Bench must take note of its strict system constraints. The application is developed exclusively for the Apple ecosystem, requiring a Mac computer equipped with Apple silicon hardware and running macOS 26 or later. Intel-based Mac workstations are completely unsupported, and cross-platform compatibility for Windows or Linux environments does not exist. Furthermore, publisher documentation notes that planned companion builds for iPhone and iPad are currently on hold. These rigid prerequisites mean that production studios operating mixed-platform hardware fleets or maintaining earlier operating systems cannot deploy Bench without significant hardware upgrades.

Licensing parameters also establish specific operational boundaries for everyday studio use. A standard license permits the software to run on only one Mac workstation at a time, limiting multi-seat parallel editing or floating studio bay configurations without active credential management. For editors working in the field, on location shoots, or during travel, the desktop software supports offline operation for up to three consecutive days before requiring network check-ins. On the security and privacy front, the publisher explicitly states that project files and video footage remain stored directly on the user's local Mac storage rather than transferring to remote cloud servers, preserving internal data governance.

These technical boundaries provide both advantages and clear hurdles. Keeping media files entirely on local storage avoids the network bandwidth bottlenecks and cloud privacy liabilities associated with browser-based editing suites. Conversely, the strict macOS 26 minimum requirement creates a significant adoption hurdle, as enterprise production facilities often deliberately lag behind current operating system releases to maintain compatibility with legacy peripheral drivers and third-party audio plugins.


## Pricing Structure, Trial Terms, and Billing Transparency

Software buyers frequently suspect questionable practices when digital services disguise trial terms or enforce automatic recurring subscriptions through hidden credit card authorizations. Evaluating the commercial terms of Bench reveals a straightforward trial policy. The publisher offers a seven-day free trial that begins the first time a user signs into the desktop client on their Mac. Critically, registration for this trial requires no payment card details whatsoever. Because no card is entered, the trial cannot automatically convert into a paid recurring bill when the seven days expire, eliminating the risk of accidental billing surprises.

For users who decide to continue using the software after testing the trial, the published commercial path is structured as a tiered monthly commitment. The publisher states an introductory rate of one pound for the initial paid month, shifting to four pounds and ninety-nine pence per month thereafter until cancelled. Subscriptions are scheduled to open formally when the software exits its private alpha phase. Prospective buyers should note that high-end Pro capabilities—specifically whole-archive search indexing across spoken dialogue and on-screen imagery—are not publicly available and are excluded from the initial free trial. The published pricing reflects an accessible consumer entry point rather than an expensive enterprise contract.

While the published monthly rate is exceptionally low compared to industry-standard creative suites, buyers must recognize that entry-level consumer pricing often correlates with community-driven support models rather than dedicated enterprise service-level agreements. Commercial studios evaluating the tool must clarify whether support channels exist for resolving production-blocking bugs during alpha testing, or whether troubleshooting is strictly self-directed.

While traditional non-linear editors occasionally integrate basic speech-to-text plugins or automated transcription generators, Bench relies on external coding engines to parse project metadata and manipulate the timeline structure. The desktop application packages setup documentation within its local interface to assist users in establishing the agent connection, effectively bridging natural language instruction with direct timeline editing controls.

Buyers must recognize the scope and technical realities of this architecture. Integration is explicitly documented for Claude Code and Codex; there is no documented support for custom local language models, open-source weights, or competing proprietary chat interfaces. Because Claude Code and Codex are external developer systems that undergo continuous upstream development, buyers must recognize that timeline synchronization depends on how stably the harness maintains compatibility across third-party API changes. ## Contrasting Bench with Traditional NLEs and Generative Video

To evaluate Bench objectively, buyers must understand where it sits in relation to traditional non-linear editors and generative artificial intelligence tools. Standard industry platforms such as Adobe Premiere Pro, Apple Final Cut Pro, and Blackmagic DaVinci Resolve provide mature post-production ecosystems with deep multi-camera synchronization, extensive plugin libraries, and hardware-accelerated color grading panels. However, these legacy applications rely almost entirely on manual user inputs or rigid, rule-based scripting, lacking the intuitive agent-directed workflows that Bench explores through its Claude Code and Codex connections.

Conversely, purely generative AI video platforms focus on synthesizing artificial video frames from scratch using text prompts. While these generative platforms demonstrate impressive conceptual rendering, they suffer from temporal instability, visual distortion, and a complete absence of granular timeline control. Bench deliberately occupies a distinct middle ground. It treats authentic footage and human creative judgment as foundational, using AI agents strictly to carry out mechanical editorial operations like trimming, ducking, and grading, while giving editors complete manual control to fine-tune every frame.

This positioning makes Bench an interesting tool for creators who reject synthetic footage generation but seek relief from mundane timeline mechanics. However, because traditional editors boast decades of codec stability, broadcast-standard color calibration, and reliable export engines, Bench should currently be regarded as an experimental editorial companion rather than a total replacement for proven post-production suites.

No. The system uses an approval-first architecture where every cut, color shift, or audio mix proposed by the agent arrives in an isolated preview state. Edits only commit to your sequence when you explicitly approve them.

### What hardware and operating system does Bench require?

Bench requires an Apple silicon Mac running macOS 26 or later. Intel-based Macs and Windows workstations are not supported, and companion builds for iPhone and iPad are currently on hold.

### Will my credit card be billed automatically when the seven-day trial concludes?

No. The publisher does not collect credit card details to start the seven-day trial. Because no payment information is on file, the trial simply concludes without automatic charges or unexpected recurring bills.

### Which specific artificial intelligence agents can control the Bench timeline?

According to the publisher documentation, Bench is built specifically to connect with Claude Code and Codex. Setup instructions are bundled directly inside the desktop application.

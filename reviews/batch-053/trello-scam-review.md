# Trello review: features, pricing and buyer considerations

**Last editorial review:** 2026-11-16  
**Category:** Visual project and task management  
**Method:** Source-led desk review; no hands-on testing is claimed.

Trello organizes work into boards, lists and cards, with plan tiers that change collaboration and automation allowances. Check whether that simple visual model is enough for the work you coordinate.

## Editorial verdict

Atlassian publishes Trello’s plan details and mobile availability. This review uses publisher documentation and does not claim personal testing or independent customer outcomes.

## Trello review: when a board is the right level of structure

Trello’s board, list and card model is easy to understand when the work moves through visible stages. Before adopting it, write down the stages the team uses and define what must be true before a card moves forward. Create a small board with a new request, active work, a waiting state and completed items. Give one card an owner, a due date and the information needed to act. Ask a colleague to explain the next step using the board alone. If the answer depends on a separate chat, improve the card or board convention.

A visual board does not automatically represent capacity, dependencies or ownership across several projects. Test what happens when work is paused, reassigned or split into two tasks. Decide whether a label or custom field clarifies the work, or whether it adds another detail people will forget to maintain. The best starting board is usually the smallest one that answers the questions the team actually asks.


## Trello boards, cards and collaboration boundaries

Use a representative project to see how people find the board they need and recognize the current version. Make card titles specific enough to scan, then put acceptance criteria and relevant links in the card itself. Try a handoff from requester to owner and then to reviewer. Verify that due dates and comments are visible to each role. A board can make work visible, so consider what information belongs on a shared card and whether guest access is appropriate.

Trello’s published Free plan lists up to 10 collaborators per Workspace and up to 10 boards. If your group is close to those limits, model the likely user count before moving work. Do not count only the people who create cards: reviewers and occasional contributors may need access too. Confirm the current definition of a collaborator and whether the plan’s limits apply per workspace, user or board.


## Trello pricing, automation and operating costs

At the time of review, Trello’s official page displayed Free at $0 and Standard at $5 per user per month when billed annually or $6 when billed monthly. Free included unlimited cards, up to 10 boards per workspace and 250 monthly command runs, along with other stated limits. Verify the current Premium and Enterprise features, automation allowance and card or attachment constraints directly before purchase. Prices are an observation from the publisher’s page, not a quote for every country or tax situation.

Estimate the bill for all active collaborators and include the cost of any add-ons or third-party tools the board requires. If automation is important, count expected runs and test a rule with both matching and non-matching cards. Make sure a teammate can tell whether the action happened and can correct an unexpected change. A small, visible automation is easier to trust than a large set of rules whose ownership no one can identify.


## Trello mobile use and work away from a desk

Atlassian lists mobile apps as part of Trello’s platform. Test the exact actions contributors will perform from a phone: opening the right board, reading a card, adding a comment, changing an owner and attaching a file. Check whether labels and card descriptions remain legible on the devices your team uses. If field staff depend on an update, verify how connectivity interruptions behave and what confirms that a change was saved.

Mobile access is useful only when the board has a clear structure and the input remains manageable. Ask a person who did not configure the board to add a new request on a small screen. If they must remember several hidden rules or hunt for the right list, simplify the workflow before announcing mobile adoption.


## Trello alternatives: compare visual boards with the full job

Compare Trello with the current system and one alternative using the same example project. Include the columns, cards, owners, due dates, files and comments your team actually needs. Observe whether people can spot blocked work and answer “what should happen next?” without a meeting. If your work needs extensive dependencies, cross-project capacity or structured reporting, ask the provider to demonstrate those exact cases rather than assuming that a board will cover them.

A spreadsheet may be sufficient for a small, stable list; a project manager may fit complex schedules; a database workspace may suit teams that need custom records and views. Compare administration, permissions, reporting and export alongside the subscription. Avoid switching just because a familiar board looks tidy in a demo; use a decision rule written before comparing services.


## Trello data access and a practical exit test

Before transferring important projects, identify workspace administrators and review which users can invite collaborators or enable integrations. Use a non-sensitive board to explore what an export contains. Check that card titles, descriptions, dates, comments and attachments are useful outside Trello. Document any fields or automations that need to be recreated elsewhere. If retention or compliance requirements apply, ask Atlassian for current product and security documentation and get the appropriate review from your organization.

Keep an independent copy of critical decisions and agree how the team will continue working during a service interruption. Remove a test user and confirm the permissions you intended to change. These steps are ordinary procurement diligence; they do not imply the product is unsafe or fraudulent.


## Who should consider Trello?

Trello can be a reasonable candidate when the work is easy to represent as cards moving through a few understood stages. Start with a small project and verify collaborator and automation limits. Teams that need a richer project model should test those requirements before migrating. Confirm current plan details, renewal and billing period at checkout, and decide who will maintain the board conventions after launch.

The public sources support a description of Trello’s board-based workflow and its published plan structure. They do not establish a universal fit or a customer’s results. Treat “SCAM Review” as a buyer-diligence heading, not a finding that Trello is fraudulent. Evaluate the service against your own needs and keep the evidence for your decision.


## Features and decision criteria

- Boards, lists and cards
- Assignees and due dates
- Plan-dependent automation, views and collaboration limits

## 2Flights.io review: a flight tracker built around your own travel history

2Flights combines a flight-status dashboard with a personal travel log. The publisher describes adding past and upcoming trips, scanning a boarding pass or forwarding a confirmation email, then viewing flight updates, airports, routes, aircraft details and cumulative travel statistics. The app also promotes notifications, home-screen widgets, iOS Live Activities and optional sharing with friends. Its pitch is broader than watching an aircraft on a map: it wants to keep a record of where the traveler has been and help coordinate where they or their friends are going next.

That mix can be useful for a frequent traveler who wants a single place for trips, alerts and travel statistics. It also means a user may give the app more than a flight number. A boarding-pass barcode can contain a passenger name, booking reference, seat and other details; a confirmation email can contain itinerary information; a social feature can reveal future plans. The right review question is not only whether the map is attractive, but also what data each import method sends, who can see a shared trip and how to remove the history later.


## Alerts are an organizing layer, not the airline’s authoritative record

The product lists notifications for departure and arrival changes, delays, cancellations, gate changes, aircraft changes and some airport information such as check-in counters or baggage claim. That breadth can reduce the need to open several airline and airport pages while traveling. The website says trips already added are cached for offline viewing, while live updates require internet access. A traveler should therefore expect the saved itinerary to remain visible during a connection gap but not assume that new disruption information will arrive without data service.

2Flights’ own Terms say it relies on third parties to provide information and does not accept liability for losses from relying entirely on that functionality. Flight schedules, gate assignments and baggage details can change, and some airport fields may not be published publicly. Treat the alert as a useful heads-up. For check-in, a rebooking, a missed connection or a time-sensitive gate change, confirm with the airline or airport display before acting. The review did not measure alert speed, flight coverage or notification reliability.


## Test the route and the notification path you will actually use

A flight-tracking app can only help if the flight is matched correctly and the alert reaches the phone in time. Before relying on it, add a completed journey and check the operating carrier, date, origin and destination against the booking. If you set up a future trip, confirm that notifications are enabled both in the app and in the operating system, and check that battery-saving settings are not suppressing background activity. These checks reduce avoidable surprises but do not turn the service into a guaranteed airline feed.

Codeshares and schedule changes are a useful test case because the marketed flight number can differ from the operating carrier’s number. If the itinerary is unclear or the app shows conflicting times, use the airline’s record as the source of truth. We did not compare 2Flights against a live flight-data feed, so no coverage percentage, refresh interval or latency claim is made here. A small route-specific trial is more informative than assuming a global marketing statement applies equally to every regional carrier.


## Boarding-pass scans and email imports save typing but deserve care

The app advertises scanning a boarding pass and forwarding a flight confirmation email as faster ways to create a trip. Its own article notes that boarding-pass barcodes can encode a passenger name, booking reference, seat and, depending on the carrier, other personal details. That article says the scan is processed locally on the device and is not uploaded or stored on the publisher’s servers without the user’s explicit consent. This is a helpful, specific statement about the scanning feature, but it is still a publisher description rather than a technical test of the current app build.

Email forwarding raises a separate question because a booking message may include passenger names, confirmation codes, ticket details and contact information. The general privacy policy describes email and name as information that may be requested and lists app providers; it does not provide a detailed field-by-field explanation for forwarded itineraries in the text reviewed. Before using the feature, read the in-app instructions, confirm the forwarding address and what is retained, and avoid forwarding messages that contain information you do not need the app to process. Delete a test import and confirm how that affects the account record.


## Friend sharing is opt-in, but an itinerary can still reveal sensitive plans

2Flights promotes a friends list, shared journey tracking and travel-stat leaderboards. The product says users can choose what is visible to friends or keep flights private, and its privacy page says it does not gather precise device location. These are useful boundaries: sharing a flight is not the same as tracking a person’s live phone location. Even so, a future itinerary can reveal when someone will be away from home, where they are going and when they expect to return.

Keep future flights private unless there is a concrete reason to share them, and review the audience setting before inviting friends. Check whether sharing is per flight or account-wide, whether a friend can reshare the itinerary, and how removing a friend affects information already seen. The public app pages describe controls at a high level but do not answer every visibility or historical-data question. No social account or share invitation was created for this review.


## Plan names and flight limits need a clear checkout check

The U.S. App Store currently lists Basic, Pro and Business purchases. Its displayed prices include Basic at $2.49 per month or $24.99 per year, Pro at $4.99 per month or $39.99 per year, and Business at $3.99 per month or $34.99 per year. The lower displayed Business price than Pro is not enough to infer which feature set is right; the plan labels alone do not explain the difference. Prices and offerings vary by storefront, and the Google Play page confirms in-app purchases without showing the same full price list in the text reviewed.

A March 2026 Google Play review said the user had paid annually but was still asked to upgrade after adding more than four flights in a month. The developer replied that the account was on Basic, which includes up to four flights monthly, and said a higher tier was needed to add more. This is one user’s report, not evidence of a general billing issue. It does reveal a concrete point to verify: check the purchased tier name and monthly flight allowance in the receipt and account screen before assuming that “annual” means unlimited. Confirm renewal and cancellation in the store that handles the payment.


## Privacy disclosures differ by surface and leave questions worth asking

The April 2025 Privacy Policy says the app may collect IP address, app-page and usage information, operating-system details, name and email. It lists Google Play Services, Mapbox and RevenueCat as third-party services, says user-provided data is retained while the app is in use and for a reasonable time afterward, and provides an email route to request deletion. Apple’s U.S. listing separately declares contact information and user content such as photos or videos may be linked to the user for app functionality; Apple says this declaration is developer-provided and not verified by Apple. Google Play states that no data is shared with third parties, that personal information may be collected, that data is encrypted in transit and that deletion can be requested.

These statements are not a full account of every data flow. A service can rely on infrastructure or payment processors while declaring no sharing under a store’s taxonomy, but a traveler should still understand what goes to each provider. Ask whether the app uploads scanned boarding-pass content, how forwarded confirmations are processed, what friend-sharing data is visible, whether flight history is used for analytics, how long backups persist after deletion and what RevenueCat receives about subscriptions. The policy’s phrase “reasonable time” is not a specific deletion schedule. No independent privacy or network audit was performed.


## Compatibility claims should be checked against the device store

The website FAQ says 2Flights runs on iOS 16 or later and Android 10 or later. The U.S. App Store listing currently says iOS 17.2 or later, and provides specific iPad, Mac and Vision compatibility requirements. That mismatch may reflect an older website FAQ, platform changes or different build support; the public sources reviewed do not explain it. The store listing is the more practical source for a current installation check on an Apple device.

The app is offered on both Android and Apple platforms, but individual functions may differ. The website highlights Live Activities and Dynamic Island on iOS; the Google Play listing describes live updates for Android 16+ users. Before relying on a widget, airport detail or alert feature, confirm the exact OS version and feature availability in the installed build. A traveler should also set notifications to an appropriate level and test them before an important trip rather than assuming every alert will bypass operating-system power or connectivity rules.


## Reviews and product claims point to active development, not a guarantee

Store pages show a sequence of feature updates such as aircraft details, airport maps, travel statistics and notification improvements. A few individual reviews on the official website and store listings praise the design and developer responsiveness; a Google Play reviewer also described confusion about a plan limit, and another older review asked about deleting a flight or missing baggage information. These are anecdotal reports, not a representative survey or a controlled service-quality comparison.

The public evidence suggests an actively maintained personal travel app with ongoing feature work. It does not establish that every airport provides the same data, that all flights have the same alert coverage or that one traveler’s experience will match a quoted review. Use store reviews as signals for questions to test, then verify your own routes, imports, plan limits and notification behavior before depending on the app for a trip.


## Who should consider 2Flights and how to compare it

2Flights may fit a traveler who wants a personal flight log, trip statistics and a social layer alongside flight notifications, especially if cross-platform availability matters. The main tradeoffs are subscription-tier limits, the need for a network connection for live updates, variability in airport data and the sensitivity of itinerary imports. If the user only needs one airline’s current boarding information, the airline’s own app may be enough; if they want broad aviation monitoring, compare the actual data coverage and use case rather than choosing by a feature checklist alone.

A careful evaluation can start with one non-sensitive, completed trip. Add it manually, compare its data with the airline’s record, inspect the notifications and statistics, then try an upcoming flight only after checking privacy settings. Confirm how to export, delete and share the trip, and note the plan allowance before paying. This review did not run those steps or compare live data quality against an airline system.


## Deletion and portability should be confirmed before importing many trips

The privacy policy provides an email route to request deletion and says information is retained while the app is in use and for a reasonable time afterward. That gives a user a contact path, but it does not state a fixed deletion period or explain how backups and shared friend views are handled. The public sources reviewed also do not establish a complete export format for a travel history. Before importing years of trips, ask how to download a copy, remove an individual journey, close the account and confirm deletion of any associated boarding-pass or email import.

A simple trial can avoid creating a large data-cleanup project: add one completed flight, confirm what appears in the history, then use the available deletion control and check what remains in shared views. If you use an email-forwarding feature, keep the original booking email in your own mailbox and treat the forwarded copy as potentially sensitive until the app explains its lifecycle. We did not submit a deletion request or verify data removal.


## 2Flights.io scam review verdict: a real app with travel-data and plan details to verify

2Flights has identifiable iOS and Android listings, a publisher website, app privacy documents, written terms and a concrete feature set for tracking and recording travel. This evidence does not substantiate calling it a scam. Its strongest value is bringing upcoming alerts, past-trip statistics and friend sharing into one personal travel tool. The practical diligence points are the Basic plan’s reported four-flight monthly allowance, region-dependent subscription prices, the difference between website and App Store compatibility statements, third-party flight-data limitations and clearer disclosure of how boarding-pass, email and social information flows through its services.

This is a source-based desk review, not an app test, flight-data audit, subscription purchase, privacy inspection or legal opinion. Official materials and store listings were reviewed November 17, 2026. Start with a low-risk trip, compare critical details with the airline, read the subscription sheet carefully and keep future flight sharing private until you understand the controls. For urgent travel decisions, confirm the final status with the airline or airport.

## 2V Modules SCAM review: first identify the kind of purchase

2V Modules markets custom product engineering, design and development services. That is a very different purchase from subscribing to a standardized application with a public feature list and fixed monthly price. A client is buying a scoped professional engagement: discovery, design, code, project coordination and, depending on the agreement, continued support. The quality of a vendor relationship therefore depends on the people assigned, the clarity of the statement of work and the evidence that the proposed team can deliver the specific product you need.

The website presents service categories and project examples, but a polished portfolio alone cannot establish delivery quality, security maturity, financial stability or fit for a particular engagement. This article uses “SCAM Review” as the directory’s review category and evaluates public evidence. It is not a finding that 2V Modules has committed fraud. The materials reviewed did not substantiate that allegation, and prospective clients should still conduct normal agency procurement diligence.


## What services 2V Modules says it provides

The official homepage describes product discovery, user-interface and user-experience design, minimum viable products, SaaS, marketplaces, mobile applications and integrations. A buyer can use this as an initial map of capability, then narrow the conversation to a concrete delivery: which user, which workflow, which platforms, what data and what would count as an accepted release. Broad capability language is not itself a project plan or proof that every service is delivered by an in-house specialist.

Ask who will do the work, which staff are employees or contractors, which languages and frameworks they propose, and which work is expected from your own team. Clarify whether strategy and discovery are a paid phase, whether designs and source code are included in the handoff, and whether cloud infrastructure, app-store fees, third-party licenses or external vendors are separate costs. A vendor can be capable and still be a poor match if responsibilities remain implicit.


## How the public case studies should be read

2V Modules publishes a directory of case studies, including a Brainie e-learning SaaS project. The Brainie page describes an exam-preparation product, a project period, product functions and a delivery team spanning software development, design, business analysis, quality assurance and project management. Such detail can help a buyer prepare useful questions about discovery, architecture, testing and collaboration. It is still a case study written by the agency, not an independent audit or a guarantee of a similar outcome for a new client.

Ask permission to contact the client or a reference who can discuss delivery, post-launch reliability and change requests. Confirm what the agency built, what the client supplied, what was out of scope and whether the described product is still active. If the work involves similar regulated data or scale, ask for evidence relevant to that risk rather than assuming a visually similar project proves equivalent engineering controls. Do not treat a testimonial as a quantified result unless its method and baseline are available.


## Team-size statements are not fully consistent

The homepage says the agency has 20+ professionals, while the company story describes a remote team that grew beyond 30. These statements could reflect different dates, definitions or parts of an organization; the public pages reviewed do not reconcile them. Team size is usually less important than the named people allocated to your delivery, but the discrepancy is a reasonable diligence question when a client needs continuity or specialized coverage.

Request a proposed team roster, role allocation, location and availability. Ask who can make architecture decisions, who reviews code, who owns QA, and how substitutions are handled if a key person leaves. Confirm whether the estimate assumes parallel work by all named roles or only a fraction of their time. For a small MVP, an extensive roster may be unnecessary; for a complex project, vague references to a large team may not guarantee the needed expertise.


## The discovery and sales process should produce a testable scope

The homepage describes a lead and discovery path in which a prospective client shares project information, then the agency responds and discusses requirements before a quote. A discovery conversation can be valuable when the problem is not yet defined. Its output should make assumptions visible: users, workflows, integrations, risks, delivery phases and the decisions that remain open. “We will build the platform” is not specific enough to compare proposals or approve a release.

Before committing to a large build, request a written discovery deliverable and a price for that phase. It should say whether you receive user flows, wireframes, technical options, a prioritized backlog, architecture recommendations, estimates and a risk register. Set an explicit stopping point where you can use the work to solicit another bid. If discovery is free, clarify what is actually included and whether it creates any obligation or license restriction.


## Pricing is quote-based; compare total project cost, not a headline estimate

I did not find a public rate card or standard price for a 2V Modules project in the materials reviewed. A custom quote is normal for agency work, because scope and staffing can differ substantially. It does mean that a prospective customer cannot compare a plan price directly from the website. Ask for assumptions and exclusions alongside the number: project management, QA, revisions, hosting, app-store work, support and third-party licenses can materially affect total cost.

Compare bids by the same deliverables and acceptance conditions. An estimate with a lower headline cost may omit user research, accessibility, security review or post-launch fixes; a higher proposal may include a wider scope or more senior staffing. Request a breakdown by phase, payment trigger, dependency, and change-request rate. Keep contingency for scope discoveries and vendor handoff. No public price or case study should be treated as a promise that your project will fit the same budget.


## The statement of work is the central buyer safeguard

A signed statement of work should define deliverables in language both sides can inspect. For a web application, specify the supported browsers, roles, key user flows, integrations, data migration, accessibility expectations, test coverage and performance targets where relevant. For a mobile product, include operating-system support, device testing, store submission responsibilities and the process for rejected builds. Attach design and technical decisions as controlled documents rather than relying on a sales-call summary.

Define how acceptance works: who reviews each milestone, how long review takes, what counts as a defect, and how rework differs from a new feature. Put change control in writing, including how estimates and dates shift when scope changes. Name project dependencies, client response obligations and escalation contacts. These terms protect both parties by reducing the chance that a dispute about an unstated expectation appears only near launch.


## IP ownership and source-code handoff must be explicit

A client should not assume that paying an invoice automatically transfers every design, source file, reusable component or third-party asset. The agreement needs to state who owns project-specific code and design, when any assignment takes effect, which pre-existing tools remain the agency’s property, and what license the client receives for those tools. It should also list open-source packages, paid libraries, fonts, icons and stock assets with their relevant terms.

Ask for an ongoing handoff rather than a final-day archive: access to the version-control repository, issue tracker, design files, build instructions, infrastructure configuration and release credentials. Confirm that the account belongs to the client and that credentials are transferred securely. Request a dependency inventory and a short runbook so another qualified team can maintain the product. If the vendor proposes proprietary components, establish the license scope, fees, continuity plan and what happens if the relationship ends.


## Security, privacy and client data need answers before discovery

The project inquiry form asks for a person’s name, company, email, phone, approximate budget and a project description. That is enough to reveal commercially sensitive information, even before a contract exists. I could not verify a working privacy-policy destination from the public materials reviewed. This does not prove there is no policy; links can move or be temporarily unavailable. It does mean a buyer should request the current notice and ask where submissions go, who can access them and how long they are kept.

For product development, ask what access controls, secure development practices, code review, dependency scanning, secrets management, backup, incident response and subcontractor rules apply. If personal or regulated data will be used, identify controller and processor roles and execute appropriate data terms before sharing a production dataset. Use synthetic data during discovery where possible. Do not paste credentials, customer records or unreleased security details into an intake form; provide a high-level brief first and use an agreed secure channel later.


## Project delivery, QA and change management

A good agency engagement makes work visible before a release candidate appears. Agree on a communication cadence, demo schedule, work-tracking system, decision log and escalation route. Every milestone should show what changed, what remains blocked and which decisions require the client. Ask whether quality assurance is performed by someone other than the feature developer and how defects are recorded and prioritized.

The Brainie case page describes a team with dedicated QA and project-management roles for that particular engagement; it does not establish that every 2V Modules project receives the same staffing. Confirm the roles and allocation in your proposal. Establish a test environment, release approval, backup and rollback plan. For integrations, test failures and duplicate events; for payments, reconcile provider events; for AI features, document model and data handling. Tailor the checklist to your risk rather than assuming a generic “QA included” line covers it.


## Post-launch support and operational ownership

A launch is the start of operations, not the end of the work. Ask what warranty period covers implementation defects, what response times apply to urgent incidents, and which tasks are billed as maintenance. Clarify monitoring, hosting, backups, security updates, dependency upgrades and app-store policy changes. If ongoing support is optional, identify the internal person who can handle routine releases and the information they will need.

Request a production handover that includes architecture, environment inventory, deployment procedure, alerting, backup-restore instructions and known limitations. Confirm whether the agency retains infrastructure access after handoff and how it is revoked. If the team is remote and distributed, understand the support hours and holiday coverage relevant to your users. None of these questions suggests a problem with 2V Modules; they make service continuity measurable and reduce the risk of becoming dependent on undocumented knowledge.


## Independent verification and reference checks

The available public portfolio is a useful starting point, but a buyer should verify the most relevant claims through direct references. Ask for a conversation with a past client whose project resembles yours in team size, data sensitivity, integration complexity and support needs. Ask what was delivered on schedule, what changed, how defects were handled and what the client would put into the contract if starting again.

Check that the legal entity named in the proposal matches the entity that will invoice and sign. Confirm the payment beneficiary, jurisdiction, tax details and dispute venue through ordinary procurement checks. Validate team identities and repository ownership where the vendor claims public work. Use a small paid discovery or pilot before making a large advance payment. These steps are common controls for any international software agency and are not an allegation that 2V Modules has misrepresented its identity.


## Alternatives and how to compare the agency model

Alternatives include hiring an in-house team, engaging a local software consultancy, using a specialist design or engineering studio, contracting independent developers, or adapting an established SaaS platform. The right route depends on how much product knowledge must stay inside the company, whether the need is ongoing, and how much management capacity the client has. A software agency may accelerate a defined build, while an in-house team may be better suited to continual iteration and domain ownership.

Compare total cost and control: who sets priorities, owns source code, maintains the system, answers security questions and resolves incidents after launch? A freelancer can be economical for a bounded task but may leave a single-person continuity risk. A no-code product can shorten time to first use but constrain data flows or customization. A larger consultancy may offer formal controls with a higher cost. Ask each candidate to price the same outcome and state the same assumptions.


## A practical low-risk way to evaluate 2V Modules

Prepare a short brief that names the user problem, one representative workflow, data categories, integrations, constraints, desired release window and what a successful pilot would demonstrate. Share no credentials or sensitive customer records. Ask 2V Modules to explain the approach, team, unknowns, delivery phases, testing, IP terms and price. Request references and the current privacy notice before submitting detailed personal or business information.

If the fit looks promising, start with a bounded discovery phase or pilot that produces a useful artifact even if you choose another supplier later. Tie payments to reviewable work, keep repository and cloud ownership under your control, and define an exit handoff.

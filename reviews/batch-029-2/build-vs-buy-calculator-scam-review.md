# Build vs. Buy Calculator review: features, pricing and buyer considerations

**Last editorial review:** 2026-10-27  
**Category:** Interactive Financial Model  
**Method:** Source-led desk review; no hands-on testing is claimed.

Build vs. Buy Calculator is a free web-based estimation widget published by Baremetrics to help software operators evaluate internal development against paid commercial subscriptions. By prompting users for engineering salaries, team size, development duration, ongoing maintenance schedules, and third-party service costs, the tool outputs projected build expenses, annual maintenance overhead, and payback horizons. While mathematically straightforward, the utility is inherently structured to steer operators toward buying external software. Teams must verify assumptions around infrastructure overhead, technical debt, and long-term licensing fees independently.

## Evaluating the Build vs. Buy Calculator Intent

Software leaders regularly confront the dilemma of whether to build internal systems or purchase vendor tools. When evaluating the Build vs. Buy Calculator published by Baremetrics, some buyers encounter aggressive search warnings or enter queries checking for deceptive claims. This investigation provides due diligence rather than an accusation of misconduct. The calculator is an interactive promotional tool designed to illustrate how internal labor costs can rapidly exceed the monthly expense of a software-as-a-service plan. Understanding its exact structure helps leaders separate sales marketing from objective cost accounting.

The interface poses a direct question regarding whether it ever makes sense to build internal tools instead of paying a monthly fee, answering that it rarely does. It asks users to specify the annual cost of an alternative subscription service alongside internal build parameters. These parameters include the headcount of engineers required, their average salary, the projected build time in weeks, and the days per month needed for maintenance. The calculator then aggregates these data points into broad financial summaries.

Because this widget is freely accessible on the publisher site, it attracts technical founders who want rapid numerical feedback on upcoming product roadmaps. However, buyers must approach the output with full awareness that the site is not an independent management consultancy. The underlying calculations are explicitly framed to persuade developers and founders to halt in-house development and purchase subscription services instead.


## Vendor Commercial Interests and Publisher Context

The Build vs. Buy Calculator is produced by Baremetrics, a provider of recurring revenue analytics, trial insights, automated reports, and recovery tooling. The publisher's business model relies entirely on software companies choosing to purchase commercial analytics subscriptions rather than maintaining custom internal data dashboards. The pricing documentation on the Baremetrics site lists distinct tiers such as Launch, Growth, and Scale, alongside specialized add-ons like Payment Recovery and Cancellation Insights.

It is important to state clearly that the publisher has a direct commercial interest in the outcome of any calculation performed on this page. By steering prospective buyers away from custom software builds, the publisher encourages reliance on external software solutions, including its own analytics platform. When the tool generates bold summary declarations warning users not to build because projects will fail to profit, it reflects the company's marketing strategy to discourage internal tooling.

This commercial relationship does not make the calculator inherently fraudulent or malicious, but it demands healthy skepticism from financial decision-makers. A tool designed by a software vendor will rarely present an objective case where building a proprietary tool yields lasting strategic value. Founders should consider the calculator as an introductory perspective on internal payroll expenses, rather than an uncompromised financial audit.


## Input Mechanics and Core Formula Scope

The core mechanism of the calculator relies on five specific input fields. On the vendor avoidance side, the user submits the annual cost of paying for an alternative commercial service. On the in-house development side, the user inputs the number of employees required, the average employee salary, the weeks needed to build the initial version, and the days per month allocated to ongoing system maintenance. These basic inputs allow the system to calculate direct baseline compensation.

From these numbers, the interface renders four main financial projections: the total upfront cost to build, the annual cost of maintenance, the net amount saved annually, and the estimated years required to reach profit. By translating development time into full-time employee salary rates, the tool quickly demonstrates how software salaries compound over weeks of initial coding and ongoing monthly maintenance schedules.

While this logic correctly highlights that engineering time is expensive, the scope of the calculation remains relatively narrow. It models engineering expense strictly as base salary divided by time increments, omitting employer taxes, equity grants, computing resources, and recruiting overhead. It also assumes that initial build timelines and maintenance estimates stay constant, which rarely reflects real-world engineering projects.


## Unmodeled Technical and Financial Realities

When engineering leaders evaluate internal builds, direct payroll is only one part of the total cost equation. The Baremetrics calculator does not document fields for cloud hosting infrastructure, data transfer fees, database licensing, continuous integration tooling, or disaster recovery provisions. In production environments, maintaining custom software introduces non-payroll operational overhead that can significantly shift budgetary projections.

Conversely, the calculator also neglects the financial risks associated with purchasing commercial software. External software pricing frequently scales with business growth, user seat counts, data volume, or tracked revenue. For instance, publisher documentation shows that Baremetrics pricing scales upward based on annual recurring revenue tiers, moving from small business ranges up through high-growth operational brackets, with optional add-ons billed monthly.

Furthermore, commercial tools may alter their pricing models, deprecate essential APIs, or fail to satisfy strict compliance and data governance standards. The simple comparison between a static annual fee and basic developer compensation ignores these complexities. A complete evaluation requires modeling both escalating software subscription tiers and variable cloud infrastructure overhead.


## Assessing Opportunity Cost and Strategic Focus

The most compelling argument presented by the calculator is the diversion of valuable technical resources. When an engineering team spends weeks building an administrative tool, custom billing portal, or reporting dashboard, those developers are not working on the company's core commercial product. This opportunity cost is often the primary reason startups choose to buy existing services.

For example, if a team assigns two senior developers to construct an analytics pipeline over twelve weeks, the company incurs both direct salary costs and the delayed delivery of user-facing platform features. If that delay prevents the acquisition of new customers or slows roadmap velocity, the true cost of building internal tools extends far beyond the calculated payroll figures.

However, this opportunity cost trade-off can run both ways. If an organization operates in a specialized vertical with proprietary data schemas that no off-the-shelf software can accommodate, forcing an external vendor into the stack can lead to messy integration workarounds. In such cases, commercial tools may absorb dozens of engineering hours simply to sanitize and bridge data, eliminating any expected productivity gains.

## Data Portability, Customization, and Export Limitations

Financial analysts and operations managers typically expect to download underlying data, audit calculation formulas, and customize modeling assumptions. The Build vs. Buy Calculator published by Baremetrics operates strictly as a client-side interface on a standard web page. It does not provide options to export results into spreadsheets, download customized financial summaries, or link directly into enterprise enterprise resource planning tools.

Users cannot alter basic formula mechanics, such as adding custom discount rates for net present value calculations or factoring in team depreciation over multi-year cycles. The calculations remain fixed within the publisher's predetermined mathematical structure. Consequently, the tool cannot easily serve as documentation for formal board approvals, corporate budgeting proposals, or capital allocation requests.

Teams needing rigorous financial auditing must translate the calculator's surface-level findings into their own internal financial models. Relying solely on the web interface leaves significant blind spots, as financial committees require visibility into the complete tax, capital expenditure, and risk profiles of both options before authorizing major commitments.

## Alternative Methodologies for Build Versus Buy Decisions

Organizations seeking comprehensive build versus buy frameworks have access to numerous methodologies outside of marketing calculators. Many financial controllers rely on Total Cost of Ownership models that encompass procurement costs, vendor management overhead, security compliance reviews, data migration, and eventual platform deprecation. These comprehensive models evaluate costs over three to five-year horizons rather than focusing solely on immediate payroll.

Another established framework is the Core versus Context analysis. In this methodology, teams assess whether the capability under consideration provides a distinct competitive advantage in the market. If an operational tool is simply context—such as general subscription analytics, transactional email routing, or human resources management—industry best practices typically favor purchasing external solutions to preserve engineering focus for core intellectual property.

Additionally, technical architects often conduct lightweight proof-of-concept tests. By spending a single sprint testing an external vendor's API alongside a barebones in-house prototype, an engineering team can gather empirical data on integration friction, feature coverage, and ongoing maintenance demands. This hands-on approach delivers significantly more actionable data than any generic web calculator can provide.


## Proposed Due Diligence and Vendor Evaluation Plan

To conduct a thorough evaluation without falling prey to oversimplified vendor messaging, technical buyers should execute a structured, step-by-step assessment plan. The first step involves gathering precise, internal baseline figures. Instead of guessing average salaries and build durations, teams should poll their lead engineers to develop low, medium, and high estimates for initial delivery, ongoing bug fixes, and third-party updates.

The second step requires documenting the full scope of vendor subscription costs across realistic growth milestones. When evaluating commercial vendors like Baremetrics, buyers should review published pricing tiers, including how fees scale with annual recurring revenue, and identify any required add-on modules such as cancellation tracking or payment recovery. Documenting these costs over a multi-year horizon reveals how vendor pricing adjusts as the business expands.

The third step involves mapping non-payroll expenses. For internal builds, calculate the cost of database instances, server compute, backup storage, and continuous monitoring tools. For external software, evaluate implementation fees, contract review costs, and developer hours required to connect internal data feeds to external vendor APIs. Consolidating these inputs into an independent spreadsheet provides an objective, customized foundation for executive decision-making.


## Final Verdict on the Build vs. Buy Calculator

The Build vs. Buy Calculator is not a deceptive scam, but an engaging promotional asset designed to frame software purchasing decisions through the lens of engineering payroll expenses. It successfully draws attention to the often-underestimated cost of dedicated developer hours and ongoing system maintenance. For early-stage founders who have never managed full-time engineering payroll, the tool offers a valuable reality check regarding how quickly custom development costs accumulate.

Nevertheless, the utility possesses clear operational limitations that prevent it from serving as an authoritative financial instrument. Its underlying assumptions do not model hosting infrastructure, vendor tier escalation, integration maintenance, or strategic intellectual property value. Furthermore, the publisher's commercial interest in selling recurring analytics software introduces an unmistakable bias toward commercial purchasing.

Buyers should view this calculator as an accessible conversation starter rather than a definitive advisory tool. It highlights key labor variables that must be respected during technical planning, but it cannot replace a rigorous, organization-specific financial evaluation. Founders and engineering leaders must calculate their own numbers, model their growth trajectories, and assess their core competitive focus independently before making critical software decisions.
